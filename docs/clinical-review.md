# Clinical review

A critical review of Rugged Speech Test from the point of view of an NHS speech and language therapist (SLT) working with young children who have little or no spoken language.

> **Please read this first.**
> This review was written by an AI model asked to take the role of a specialist NHS SLT and to be critical. It is a **structured self-review to find problems early**. It is **not** a clinical assessment, not a sign-off, and not the opinion of a registered clinician. Guidance named below is referred to **in general terms** and must be checked against the current published documents by a real clinician. A registered SLT (HCPC-registered, ideally with AAC experience) should review the vocabulary, the defaults and the wording before any child relies on the app. The sign-off block at the end is deliberately blank.

## What was reviewed

The starter vocabulary and how it is organised; the child's screens; how speech is produced and controlled; the games; My body; the traffic light; the Parent Mode and School Mode features; the set-up journey; safeguarding and privacy; the wording used throughout; and the guides.

## Principles used

These are widely accepted in AAC practice in the UK. Each should be checked against current RCSLT and NHS England documents.

1. **AAC is for everyone who needs it, early, with no prerequisites.** It does not wait for a child to "earn" it, and it does not replace speech: it supports it.
2. **Multimodal communication.** Gesture, signing, symbols, objects and speech all count. A device is one tool among several.
3. **Person-centred and shared.** The child, family and everyone who supports them take part in choosing and learning it. Training the people around the child matters as much as the device.
4. **Modelling (aided language input).** Partners use the system themselves while they talk, so the child sees how it works.
5. **Expectant delay.** Partners wait, and give time.
6. **Core vocabulary first, with fringe vocabulary for what matters to the child.** A small number of very frequent words that go with everything, then personal words.
7. **A stable layout.** Buttons stay where they are, so the child can build motor memory (motor planning).
8. **A range of communicative functions**, not just requesting: protesting and refusing, commenting, greeting, asking, sharing, and saying how you feel.
9. **Errorless, supportive learning.** Practice is play, not a test. A wrong answer is information, not a failure, and help is offered before frustration.
10. **Consent, capacity and the child's voice.** Decisions follow the Mental Capacity Act and the child's wishes, and the child is not spoken for or over.
11. **Safe and private.** Data protection by design, the ICO Children's Code, a DPIA where needed, and clinical safety practice (DCB0129 and DCB0160 where the product is used in NHS services).
12. **Accessible.** Switch, eye-gaze and keyboard access, and clear contrast and text.

## Summary

The app's design is strong on **stability of layout, partner-friendly features, no pressure, privacy, and refusing to speak without a press**. Its main clinical risks are not in the software: they are in **how it is introduced, who chooses the words, and how well the people around the child are trained to use it**. The software cannot fix those, and its documentation now says so plainly.

## Findings, and what was done

### 1. Vocabulary

**Good.** Pages hold nine things or fewer, in large buttons. There is a stable, small core (*I, want, like, more, no, yes, help, go, my, feel, stop, look, all done, again, open, that, not, what*) and folders for the things a young child talks about. Words are plain and short (a test checks length, and that no later-stage word has crept in). No word has a stage set, so the app does not pretend to decide a curriculum.

**Concerns.**

- The core is **smaller than many SLTs would choose**, and several very high-frequency words are missing (*it, in, on, off, put, get, make, here, there, different, same*). They were left out because the brief was a small, early set; a clinician should decide which to add.
- *feel* and *my* are weak, abstract core words at the first level, and sit where they do for historical reasons (a layout that children may already have learned must not move). They could be swapped for *stop* and *look* on a **new** device.
- *Feelings* is limited on the Talk page (seven words). The fuller Feelings screen has more.
- **Fringe vocabulary is for the family**, and the app offers photos and recorded voices to make it personal. That is right, and it needs the family to use it.

**Done.** Reduced the starter set to a lean first-words set and removed words that were too advanced. Added *again, open, that, not* and *what?* as the most frequent missing words. Added a test that keeps pages small and words simple.

**For a clinician.** Choose the core. Decide whether to use **word stages** to bring words in. Check the words against the child's own communication, culture and language at home.

### 2. Symbols and colour

**Good.** A single, consistent, original drawn set (no mixing of styles), or emoji if preferred; pictures and words together by default; colour that follows word class (a Modified Fitzgerald-style key), which can be adjusted, with a warning when two classes look alike.

**Concerns.**

- The drawn symbols are **new and untested with children.** Iconicity (does a child recognise it?) is a matter for testing, not opinion. Some are less obvious than others (*want, not, that, feel, tummy*).
- Colour coding for word class is useful for some children and neutral for others; changing colours can undermine it, which is why the app warns.
- Emoji vary between computers and are sometimes less clear; some have no drawing and fall back to emoji.

**Done.** A choice between drawn symbols and emoji, at set-up and in settings; pictures-only and words-only options; real **photographs** for people and places, which are usually the most meaningful.

**For a clinician.** Test the symbols with the child. Replace any the child does not recognise with a photograph.

### 3. Speech output

**Good.** The voice is chosen with the child, any time. **Speed, pitch and volume** can be set, with named starting points. A sentence can be read out **one word at a time, lighting each word**, which supports comprehension and modelling. **Pronunciation** of names can be fixed without changing the spelling. **Nothing is spoken unless a person presses something**, which matters especially for people who stammer, because being spoken over is a core harm. **Give me time** says "I know what I want to say. Please give me a moment."

**Concerns.**

- **Voice quality.** The voices are those Windows provides. Adult-sounding voices for young children can feel wrong, and a child may reject a voice they do not identify with. The app cannot supply a child voice, and neural voices were deliberately not built (they would add a large download and a separate licence). A **recorded voice** per button (a parent's, a sibling's) is the app's answer, and works well for key words.
- **Press mode default.** Many early communicators need **immediate speech on a press** so they learn cause and effect. The built-in default (add to the sentence, then Speak) suits a child who already builds sentences.

**Done.** The set-up now asks, with an explanation and "say it straight away" first. The Access tab explains the options.

**For a clinician.** Choose the press mode and the voice with the child. Consider recorded voices for the first ten words.

### 4. Learning and practice (games)

**Good.** No clock, no score, no wrong-answer sound, no motion, nothing spoken unless pressed. Find-the-word, Snap and the Rollercoaster all work from the child's own vocabulary, so practice uses words they have. The Rollercoaster builds sentences from two to four words, a recognised way to extend utterance length.

**Concerns.**

- **Testing.** "Which word is this?" can feel like being tested, and some children refuse tasks that feel like tests. Games are play, and are not on the default top bar.
- Praise: evaluative praise ("Well done") is not always helpful and can feel patronising to older children.
- Games should **not** be used as a substitute for functional communication in real situations.

**Done.** Find-the-word and the Rollercoaster **point to the right word after two wrong tries**, so a child is never left stuck. "Well done" was replaced with neutral wording ("All done. Now …"). Game speech is **not** kept in Recent history or the activity log, so practice does not look like communication.

**For a clinician.** Decide whether games suit the child, and keep them to a small part of the day.

### 5. My body

**Good.** Pointing to a body part and saying how it feels and how much is useful for children who cannot say where it hurts, and a figure that looks like the child (including a wheelchair and the equipment they use) supports identification. The equipment can be pointed to ("My pump is not working").

**Concerns.**

- It is a **communication aid, not a pain assessment.** A child's pointing is not a diagnosis, and adults must not treat it as one. Pain assessment in children with complex needs uses its own tools.
- **Safeguarding.** The figure is always dressed and the private area is only ever "under my pants" (a phrase aligned with the widely used "pants" approach to teaching children that their private parts are private). A child may use it to disclose. The app **does not** log, flag or notify, by design, so adults must know what to do.
- The figure's choice of **boy, girl or non-binary** and a wheelchair are only options; a child's own identity should lead.

**Done.** Documented plainly in both guides. A test checks that no anatomical words appear in any label.

**For a clinician and the setting's safeguarding lead.** Agree in writing how adults respond to a disclosure *before* it is used. Agree who may change the figure.

### 6. The traffic light

**Good.** It lets a child control **how much they are spoken to**, without words, and respects their autonomy. It is partner-facing: adults can see it. Words can be edited.

**Concerns.** It only works if adults respect it. It should not be used to manage behaviour.

**For a clinician.** Introduce it with partners, with examples.

### 7. Parent Mode and School Mode

**Good.** All complexity is behind the PIN. Targets, notes, a handover sheet and a review report support multi-professional working and annual reviews. The activity log supports looking at **how much** the device is used through the day, week and month. It is off by default and can be cleared.

**Concerns.**

- **Counting use can mislead.** More presses are not better communication. The log must not be used to judge a child or a family, or to set targets for "number of presses".
- A log of what a child said is **sensitive**. It needs a legal basis, a retention period, and a DPIA in a school.
- **Outcome measures are absent.** The app does not measure communication progress and should not claim to.

**Done.** The report wording describes use, not progress. Retention, a clear button and an off switch are built in. The documentation says what the log is for and what it is not for.

**For a clinician.** Use established, validated outcome and goal-setting measures, and treat device data as one small input.

### 8. Introducing the device, and the people around the child

**Concern.** The most common reason AAC fails is **not the device**: it is that partners are not trained, the device is not with the child, words are not updated, or it is used only for requests. The app can support this but cannot do it.

**Done.** Both guides teach modelling, waiting, starting small and keeping buttons still, and say plainly that the starter words are a beginning.

**For a clinician.** Offer training. Agree a plan for introducing it, and for who updates the words.

### 9. Wording and tone

**Good.** British English, no jargon on the child's screens, and no deficit framing ("What I need", "too loud in here"). Sensory needs are things to be met, not problems.

**Concern.** A few adult-facing words ("hold-to-select", "scanning") are specialist; they are explained where they appear.

### 10. Accessibility

**Good.** Switch scanning (one or two switches), hold-to-select for eye-gaze or head-pointer users, repeat-press suppression, keyboard control, high contrast, text size, a low-arousal palette, and colour schemes with a readability check.

**Gaps.** Not tested with a screen reader or with real eye-gaze equipment. Windows' own eye control and accessibility features may be needed alongside it.

### 11. Safety and governance

**Good.** Nothing leaves the device. There are no accounts. Backup is explicit. A strict security design is checked by automated tests ([security notes](security.md)).

**Gaps.** No DPIA, no clinical safety case (DCB0129) or hazard review with a clinical safety officer, no DTAC assessment, no registration decision with the MHRA about whether it is a medical device (a communication aid is usually not, but claims matter), and no testing on real Windows hardware. These are **required before NHS use**.

## Changes made because of this review

- A **press-mode choice** at set-up, with the immediate-speech option first and explained.
- A **hint after two wrong tries** in Find-the-word (the Rollercoaster already had one).
- **Neutral wording** in First / Then ("All done. Now …").
- Practice speech (games) is **kept out of the history and activity log**.
- A **leaner, plainer starter vocabulary**, with a few missing very-common words added, and tests to keep it that way.
- **Drawn symbols or emoji**, and pictures-only or words-only, at set-up and in settings.
- The guides now teach **modelling, waiting and starting small**, and state the safeguarding position plainly.
- A **handover sheet** and a **review report** that describe use, not progress.

## Questions for a clinician

1. Which words belong in the first page for this child, and which in later stages?
2. Is the press mode right? Should the first words have a recorded voice?
3. Do the drawn symbols work for this child, or are photographs better?
4. Is the voice acceptable to the child?
5. Are games and the piano appropriate, and how much?
6. Who may change the vocabulary, and how are changes shared with the people around the child?
7. How will progress be measured, using established tools?
8. What is the plan for training partners and for reviewing the device?
9. Is the wording on My body, the traffic light and Help right for this child and this setting?
10. Are any phrases culturally or linguistically wrong for the family?
